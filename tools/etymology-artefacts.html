  Betelgeuse

The most famous scribal error in the sky. The initial ya was read as ba, the two letters differing only in where the dot sits, giving Bat al-Jawza', which means nothing in any language, and then Bedalgeuze in the Alfonsine Tables of about 1250.

The same figure as Betelgeuse, at the other end. Orion's two brightest stars are the hand and the foot of one body that Arab astronomers saw where the Greeks saw a hunter.

Algol

│ رأس الغول

├ Latin · medieval
│ Algol
│
└ English · now
  Algol, and ghoul, the same word

An eclipsing variable that dims every 2.87 days, sitting in the severed head of Medusa in the Greek figure and carrying a demon's name in the Arabic one. The English ghoul is the same word.

Not settled: Whether anyone noticed the variability before Montanari in 1667 is genuinely disputed. The names are suggestive and suggestion is not evidence.

Saturn

Latin · unknown · Saturnus

┌ Babylon
│ Kajamanu, the steady one
│
├ Persia
│ Keyvan, the same word, still in use
│ کیوان
│
├ Greece · 4th c. BC
│ Kronos, before that Phainon, the shining
│ Κρόνος
│
├ Rome
│ Saturnus
│
└ English · now
  Saturn, and Saturday

The Romans linked it to serere, to sow, which is folk etymology. Persian still calls the planet Keyvan, straight from Babylonian Kajamanu, the steady one, a name for its slowness that has been in use for about 2,500 years.

Not settled: Probably Etruscan, from Satre, but the Roman derivation from sowing is a guess made after the fact.

Jupiter

Latin · sky father · Iuppiter

┌ Babylon
│ Marduk, the city god of Babylon
│
├ Greece · 4th c. BC
│ Zeus, taking the Babylonian assignment
│ Ζεύς
│
├ Rome
│ Iuppiter, from dyeus phter, sky father
│
├ and sideways, into the week
│ dies Iovis  to  jeudi, giovedi, jueves
│
└ North · c.400
  Thor's day, and so Thursday

The oldest name in the sky. It is the same phrase as Greek Zeus pater and Sanskrit Dyaus Pitar, all three descended from one reconstructed form, and it is why Thursday and jeudi are the same day named twice: once by translating the god into Thor, once by keeping him.

Mars

Latin · unknown · Mars, older Mavors

┌ Babylon
│ Nergal, god of plague
│
├ Greece · 4th c. BC
│ Ares, before that Pyroeis, the fiery one
│ Ἄρης
│
├ Rome
│ Mars
│
├ and sideways, into the week
│ dies Martis  to  mardi, martes, martedi
│
└ North · c.400
  Tiw's day, and so Tuesday

The god of war, matched to Babylonian Nergal, god of plague. The Greeks had called the planet Pyroeis, the fiery one, for its colour, and half the world's names for it still describe the colour rather than the god.

Not settled: The root of Mars itself is not known. An Etruscan origin is often proposed and cannot be shown.

Pleiades

Greek · disputed · Pleiades · Πλειάδες

Greek     Pleiades     ─┐
Arabic    al-Thurayya  ─┤
Sanskrit  Krittika     ─┼──▶
Japanese  Subaru       ─┤
Maori     Matariki     ─┘

  no shared root: five traditions named it five times, and four
  of them counted seven where six are all most people see

The best-named object in the sky, because almost every tradition named it separately: Subaru in Japanese, gathered together; Krittika in Sanskrit, the first of the 27 nakshatras; Matariki in Maori, which opens the year; al-Thurayya in Arabic, the abundant one. What they share is the count: nearly everyone says seven, and six is all most people can see.

Not settled: The Greek name has been derived from plein, to sail, from pleios, full, and from the mother Pleione. None is settled.

Milky Way

Greek, through Latin · milky circle · galaxias kyklos · γαλαξίας κύκλος

Greek     galaxias kyklos, milky circle       ─┐
Latin     via lactea, the same picture        ─┤
Chinese   yinhe, the silver river             ─┼──▶
Sanskrit  Akashaganga, the Ganges of the sky  ─┘

  milk in Europe, a river almost everywhere else

Calqued into Latin as via lactea and then into every European language, while galaxias itself gave the word galaxy. Elsewhere it is usually a river or a road: Chinese yinhe, the silver river, and Sanskrit Akashaganga, the Ganges of the sky.
